HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE
1984

What is BB84?

The world's first quantum cryptographic protocol, published in 1984 by Charles H. Bennett and Gilles Brassard.

BB84 allows two parties — conventionally called Alice (sender) and Bob (receiver) — to establish a cryptographically secure shared secret key over an insecure quantum channel. Crucially, any attempt by a third party (Eve) to intercept the key inevitably disturbs the quantum states and is detectably revealed through an elevated error rate.

Unlike classical encryption schemes such as RSA or Diffie-Hellman, BB84's security does not depend on any hard mathematical problem. It relies entirely on the laws of quantum physics — specifically the no-cloning theorem and Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle. This makes it unconditionally secure, provably unbreakable even by a quantum computer running Shor's or Grover's algorithm.

Why Do We Need QKD?

Modern internet security is built on computational hardness — the assumption that certain mathematical problems take an astronomically long time to solve on classical computers. This assumption is under threat.

Peter Shor's 1994 quantum algorithm can factor large integers exponentially faster than any classical algorithm. Once sufficiently large quantum computers exist, RSA-2048, ECC, and most public-key cryptosystems will be broken.

STEP 1

Alice Prepares Quantum States

Alice generates a random sequence of bits (0s and 1s) and encodes each bit into a photon using a randomly chosen basis — either Rectilinear (+) or Diagonal (×). She fires these photons one by one down the quantum channel toward Bob.

Key Principle: The chosen encoding basis is kept secret from Bob.

STEP 2

Bob Measures the Photons

Bob randomly picks a measurement basis (+) or (×) for each arriving photon — independently of Alice. If he picks the same basis as Alice, his measurement perfectly recovers the original bit. If he picks the wrong basis, quantum mechanics forces a random, uncorrelated outcome (50/50 chance).

Key Principle: Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle — you cannot simultaneously know incompatible observables.

STEP 3

Basis Sifting (Public Comparison)

Alice and Bob compare only their chosen bases over a classical (public) channel — not the actual bit values. They discard all photons where their bases differed (~50% of transmissions). The remaining photons form the Sifted Key.

This basis announcement is perfectly safe — knowing the basis gives Eve zero information about the actual bit value.

STEP 4

QBER Estimation (Eve Detection)

Alice and Bob publicly reveal a small, randomly chosen sample of their sifted key bits and compare them. If the Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER) exceeds ~12.9%, Eve is statistically almost certainly present — they abort the protocol entirely.

QBER = (Error bits in sample) / (Total sample bits) × 100%

⚠️ Eve's measurements collapse quantum states, introducing unavoidable ~25% QBER.

STEP 5

Error Correction (Information Reconciliation)

Even without Eve, real optical channels introduce noise. Alice and Bob run an error correction protocol over the classical channel to make their keys identical without revealing the key itself. Two approaches are supported:

  • Cascade Protocol: Block-based binary bisection with interactive parity checks and multi-pass correction.
  • LDPC Syndrome Decoding: Tanner Graph belief propagation — used in modern communications.
STEP 6

Privacy Amplification

Even if Eve captured a few bits during error correction, she may hold partial information about the key. Privacy amplification uses a random Toeplitz hash function to compress the key into a shorter one — mathematically destroying all partial information Eve accumulated.

Final Key Length ≈ Sifted Key Length × (1 − 2 × QBER)

Threat Model: Who is Eve?

Intercept-Resend Attack

Eve intercepts each photon, measures it in a random basis, then resends a new photon to Bob. She correctly guesses the basis ~50% of the time. The other 50% cause errors. Expected QBER ≈ 25%, well above the abort threshold of 12.9%.

Man-in-the-Middle (MITM)

Eve poses as Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob. BB84 alone does not prevent MITM — it requires an authenticated classical channel (typically a pre-shared secret or certificate authority) to bind identities.

Why Eve Can Never Copy a Qubit

The No-Cloning Theorem (Wootters & Zurek, 1982) proves that it is physically impossible to create an exact copy of an unknown quantum state. Eve cannot "copy and forward" — she must choose to measure, inevitably disturbing the state.

Quantum Physics Primer for BB84

Before running the simulation, you need to understand the quantum mechanical concepts that make BB84 both work and remain secure.

BB84 transmits classical bits (0 and 1) by encoding them into quantum states of individual photons. The security of the protocol follows directly from a few fundamental laws of quantum mechanics that have no classical analogue.

1. The Qubit — A Quantum Bit

Classical computers use bits — binary values that are deterministically either 0 or 1. Quantum computers (and quantum communication systems like BB84) use qubits, which obey the laws of quantum mechanics.

A qubit can exist in a superposition of both |0⟩ and |1⟩ simultaneously, described by a complex linear combination:

$$ |\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle \quad \text{where} \quad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 $$

Here, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex probability amplitudes. $|\alpha|^2$ gives the probability of measuring |0⟩ and $|\beta|^2$ gives the probability of measuring |1⟩. The constraint $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$ ensures probabilities sum to 100%.

Classical Bit

State ∈ {0, 1} — deterministic, copyable, readable without disturbance.

Qubit

State ∈ Complex unit sphere — probabilistic, non-copyable, measurement disturbs the state.

Bloch Sphere Representation

Any qubit state $|\psi\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$ can be visualised as a point on the surface of the unit Bloch Sphere. The poles represent |0⟩ and |1⟩, while the equator represents equal superpositions like $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$.

2. Encoding Bits In Photon Polarisation

In BB84, qubits are physically implemented as photon polarisation states. The direction of a photon's electric field oscillation (its polarisation angle) encodes the qubit state. Alice chooses between two incompatible measurement bases:

The two bases are mutually unbiased — measuring a + photon with the × basis gives a completely random result (50% chance of 0, 50% chance of 1), carrying no information about the original bit.

3. Superposition

A quantum state can exist in a superposition of multiple classical states simultaneously. For example, the diagonal basis states are superpositions of the rectilinear states:

$$ |{+}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle \qquad |{-}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle $$

This is not a classical "either/or" — it is a genuine physical superposition. A photon in the $|+\rangle$ state is not "secretly" horizontal or vertical — it has no definite polarisation until it is measured in a basis that resolves it.

In BB84: Alice sends photons in superposition states from the diagonal basis. If Bob measures in the wrong basis, he collapses the photon to a random state — he might get 0 or 1 with equal probability regardless of which bit Alice intended. This randomness is irreducible and fundamental.

4. Measurement & Wavefunction Collapse

Measuring a quantum state irrevocably collapses its superposition. Before measurement, a photon prepared in $|+\rangle$ has no definite polarisation. The moment Bob measures it using the rectilinear (+) basis, the wavefunction collapses to either $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ with equal probability.

$$ P(\text{measure }0 | \text{state }|{+}\rangle, \text{basis }+) = |\langle 0|{+}\rangle|^2 = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{2} $$

This is not a limitation of our measuring instruments — it is a fundamental law. There is no way to measure a qubit without causing collapse unless you measure in its exact preparation basis.

5. Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle states that certain pairs of physical observables cannot both be known to arbitrary precision simultaneously. In quantum information, this generalises to mutually unbiased observables — measuring one gives you maximum information about itself but destroys all information about its complementary observable.

$$ \sigma_x \cdot \sigma_p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2} $$

In the BB84 context: measuring a photon in the rectilinear basis tells you nothing about what you would have gotten if you had measured it in the diagonal basis — and vice versa. These two measurements are complementary / mutually unbiased.

Why This Stops Eve: To eavesdrop, Eve must intercept and measure the photon. But she doesn't know which basis Alice used — so she guesses and measures in the wrong basis ~50% of the time. This collapses the photon's state incorrectly, and when she re-sends a new photon to Bob, he notices the errors. The Uncertainty Principle makes this unavoidable.

6. The No-Cloning Theorem

The No-Cloning Theorem (Wootters & Zurek, 1982; Dieks, 1982) proves that it is physically impossible to make a perfect copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state. This is a direct consequence of the linearity of quantum mechanics.

$$ \nexists \; U : U(|\psi\rangle \otimes |0\rangle) = |\psi\rangle \otimes |\psi\rangle \quad \forall |\psi\rangle $$

This means Eve cannot intercept a photon, copy it perfectly, send the original onward to Bob, and then safely measure the copy at her leisure. Any measurement she makes must disturb the original state, leaving a detectable trace.

Classical Information

Can be copied without limit. Reading a file doesn't alter it.

Quantum Information

Cannot be copied. Reading (measuring) always disturbs the original — this is a fundamental law, not a technology limitation.

Theoretical Foundations

Technical Appendix

Formal mathematical and cryptographic foundations for the protocols used in the quantum-secure pipeline.

This appendix contains the deep technical specifications for the classical algorithms utilized in the BB84 post-processing phase. These mechanisms ensure that the final key is consistent, secret, and properly authenticated.

1. Information Reconciliation & Privacy Amplification

The Reconciliation Problem

No quantum channel is perfect. Thermal noise and detector efficiency introduce errors. Alice and Bob must correct these WITHOUT Eve learning the final bits. We use parity checks—revealing only 1 bit of information (even/odd) to fix multiple mismatches via the Cascade or LDPC protocols.

Privacy vs. Secrecy

Eve may have intercepted a fraction of photons. During Error Correction, she hears the parity results publicly. Privacy Amplification compresses the key using universal hashing (Toeplitz matrices) so that any information Eve had disappears into the entropy of the hash.

2. Low-Density Parity-Check (LDPC) Codes

LDPC codes are highly efficient linear block codes used to correct errors over noisy channels. In BB84, they allow Alice and Bob to reconcile their keys with minimal information leakage.

The Parity-Check Matrix ($H$)

An LDPC code is defined by a sparse parity-check matrix $H$, which contains a very small number of $1$s relative to $0$s. A bit-vector $x$ is a valid codeword if and only if:

$$ Hx^T = \mathbf{0} \pmod 2 $$

Code Rate and Matrix Construction

To establish a secure key, the LDPC code must be constructed such that the Code Rate ($R$) satisfies the privacy bounds established during parameter estimation (Stage 5). For a matrix $H$ with dimensions $(n-k) \times n$:

$$ R = \frac{k}{n} $$

$n$: Codeword length | $k$: Message length

Typically, matrices are generated using Gallager's method or Progressive Edge Growth (PEG) to maximize the girth of the Tanner Graph, avoiding short cycles that degrade Belief Propagation performance.

The Tanner Graph Representation

LDPC codes are often visualized as bipartite graphs called Tanner Graphs. The graph consists of two sets of nodes:

  • Variable Nodes ($v_i$): Represent each bit of the transmitted message.
  • Check Nodes ($c_j$): Represent the parity constraints defined by the rows of $H$.

An edge $(v_i, c_j)$ exists if the $i$-th bit is part of the $j$-th parity check (i.e., $H_{j,i} = 1$).

Decoding: Belief Propagation

The Sum-Product Algorithm (SPA) is an iterative process where nodes exchange "messages" representing their current belief about a bit's value. In each iteration:

$$ L(v_i \to c_j) = L(v_i) + \sum_{k \in N(v_i) \setminus \{j\}} L(c_k \to v_i) $$

Message from variable node $i$ to check node $j$

This process repeats until all check nodes are satisfied or a maximum iteration limit is reached.

3. The Hoeffding Principle

Alice and Bob sample a random subset of their sifted bits to estimate the Error Rate (QBER). According to the Hoeffding Inequality, the probability that the estimated error rate $\hat{e}$ deviates from the true error rate $e$ by more than $\epsilon$ decreases exponentially with the sample size $n$:

$$ P(|\hat{e} - e| \ge \epsilon) \le 2e^{-2n\epsilon^2} $$

4. ChaCha20 Stream Cipher

ChaCha20 is a modern, high-speed stream cipher designed by Daniel J. Bernstein. It is an ARX (Add-Rotate-Xor) cipher, meaning it uses only simple CPU operations that run in constant time, protecting against timing attacks.

The 512-bit State

The state is organized as a $4 \times 4$ matrix of 32-bit words:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} C_0 & C_1 & C_2 & C_3 \\ K_0 & K_1 & K_2 & K_3 \\ K_4 & K_5 & K_6 & K_7 \\ N_0 & N_1 & N_2 & N_3 \end{pmatrix} $$

$C$: Constants | $K$: 256-bit Key | $N$: Nonce & Counter

The Quarter-Round Function

The core of ChaCha20 is the quarter-round $QR(a, b, c, d)$:

$$ \begin{aligned} a &= a + b; & d &= (d \oplus a) \lll 16 \\ c &= c + d; & b &= (b \oplus c) \lll 12 \\ a &= a + b; & d &= (d \oplus a) \lll 8 \\ c &= c + d; & b &= (b \oplus c) \lll 7 \end{aligned} $$

ChaCha20 performs 20 rounds (10 column rounds and 10 diagonal rounds) to ensure complete diffusion of the key and nonce into the keystream.

5. Poly1305 Authentication (MAC)

While ChaCha20 ensures confidentiality, Poly1305 provides authenticity. It is a Wegman-Carter MAC that proves the message has not been tampered with by an active adversary (Eve).

Prime Modulus

Poly1305 operates on 128-bit blocks and relies on the properties of a large prime number:

$ p = 2^{130} - 5 $

Authentication Mapping

The message $M$ is interpreted as a polynomial $P(x)$, and the tag is computed by evaluating it at a secret point $r$:

$$ \text{Tag} = ((\sum_{i=1}^k m_i r^{k-i+1}) \pmod p) + s \pmod{2^{128}} $$

$m_i$: message blocks | $r, s$: derived from the shared secret

Integration with BB84

In a real-world QKD link, the Final Key generated by BB84 is divided into sub-keys. Half of the bits are used as the Key for ChaCha20, and the other half serve as the One-Time Key for Poly1305, ensuring Information-Theoretic Security (ITS) for the encryption itself.
